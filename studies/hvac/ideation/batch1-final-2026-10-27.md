- Don't: Ray's words (AVOID THIS, STOP DOING THIS) — those are his.

**3. Video brief**

Nine everyday thermostat habits that make a furnace run harder, cycle more or overheat, each with what it costs and the better setting. 1) Cranking it to 80 to warm up faster: a single-stage furnace delivers heat at one rate (two-stage and modulating ones don't reach the set point faster either), so it only runs longer and overshoots; set the temperature you want. 2) Fan set to ON instead of AUTO all winter: the blower runs 24 hours a day, so older single-speed motors wear out sooner (a blower motor runs about $300-$1,100 installed for a standard motor, $600-$1,500+ for a variable-speed ECM) and the bill rises; use AUTO unless the blower is variable-speed. 3) A thermostat on an outside wall, near a lamp, a TV, a sunny window or a supply vent: false readings cause short cycling; move it or shield it. 4) Constant up-and-down adjusting through the day: every start is the hardest moment for the igniter and blower; pick a schedule and leave it. 5) Short cycling from a cycle-rate or swing setting that's too tight on digital thermostats: check the installer menu (cycles per hour) against the furnace manual. 6) Closing the vents and doors of unused rooms to 'save' heat: raises static pressure, the heat exchanger runs hot and the high limit trips; keep at least 80-90% of supply vents open. 7) Letting thermostat batteries die: the heat can drop out on the coldest night; replace them every fall ($5). 8) A smart thermostat with no C-wire 'stealing' power: can make some furnaces chatter or short cycle; add a C-wire or adapter ($20-$30). 9) Shutting the heat off completely when away in deep cold: the real risk is frozen pipes and a cold house if the furnace won't restart; a 7-10°F setback is the safe way to save (the DOE says a 7-10°F setback for 8 hours a day saves as much as 10% a year, and that a furnace 'working harder to recover' is a misconception). Habits, not repairs; no item numbers in the title.

Safety: if you smell gas, leave the house and call the gas company from outside.

- Text: $30 over the left filter, $5 over the right (white numbers)
- Don't: Red X and green check marks (we never use them); brand names.

**3. Video brief**

The filter's main job is protecting the blower and heat exchanger, and a plain pleated MERV 8 filter (about $5-$8 each in a multipack) does that while letting air through. A dense 1-inch 'allergen' or MERV 11-13 filter at $20-$35 adds resistance: in studies it cut airflow by roughly 7-11% on common single-speed (PSC) blowers, and on systems with small or few return grilles that can push the furnace toward overheating and high-limit trips, and in summer a frozen AC coil. The EPA recommends MERV 13 or as high as the system fan and filter slot can handle, so check the furnace manual or ask a tech. The worst case is a premium filter left in too long. The cheapest fiberglass filters ($1-$3) are the other extreme: they stop big debris but let fine dust coat the blower wheel and coil. How to choose: check the furnace manual for the recommended MERV; MERV 8 for most homes; change 1-inch filters every 1-3 months (monthly with pets); if you want cleaner air for allergies, a 4-5 inch media cabinet (filters about $25-$60, last 6-12 months) or a standalone HEPA room purifier ($100-$250) does it without choking the furnace. Honest limits: a high-MERV filter is fine on a system built for it; the point is matching the filter to the system. A blower motor replacement runs about $300-$1,100 (variable-speed ECM $600-$1,500+); a heat exchanger failure often means a new furnace.

## 11. THIS Is Why Your Heating Bill Is So High (The $20 Leak Hiding in Your Basement)

**1. Idea**
- Format: exaggerated source
- Built from: "THIS is Why Your Heat Bill is So High! Rim Joist Insulation Technique!" · 5,842,952 (Home RenoVision DIY, Dec 2024)

**2. Thumbnail**
- Reference:
  - Home RenoVision DIY, "THIS is Why Your Heat Bill is So High! Rim Joist Insulation Technique!" (5.8M views): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J95l9Ue4224
- Keep: The basement wood joists above the concrete wall, red arrows.
- Change: One spot only: a gap above the concrete wall with frost on the wood and a thin line of daylight coming through. One red arrow on the gap. Roger on the right, serious, pointing at it. No insulation shown.
- Text: Red band: YOUR BILL LEAKS HERE
- Don't: Dollar signs and several arrows (keep it to one).

**3. Video brief**

The leak is the rim joist (band joist): the wooden band sitting on top of the foundation wall around the basement or crawlspace, often with no insulation and gaps at the sill plate, wire and pipe holes. Warm air rises and escapes at the top of the house, and this stack effect pulls cold air in low, through those gaps, so the furnace keeps reheating outside air. ENERGY STAR estimates homeowners can save an average of 15% on heating and cooling costs (about 11% of total energy costs) by air sealing and insulating attics, floors over crawlspaces and basement rim joists; the script attributes this to the whole package, never to the rim joist alone. The fix: canned spray foam (about $5-$10 a can) in the gaps and holes, or rigid foam board cut to fit each joist bay and sealed with foam around the edges ($20-$60 for a typical section, more for a whole house); a pro spray-foam job runs much higher. How to find it: on a cold windy day, feel along the top of the basement wall or use an incense stick. Safety: if the furnace or water heater is an older natural-draft type (a metal vent into a chimney), sealing the basement tighter can make it backdraft carbon monoxide (ENERGY STAR warns of this): have a combustion safety (draft) test done and keep working CO alarms. Canned foam is flammable while curing: no flames or pilot lights nearby, ventilate. Around flues use sheet metal and high-temperature fire-rated sealant, never foam; many codes require exposed foam board to be covered with a fire-rated barrier; gloves and eye protection.

Seven cheap fixes for the drafts that make a house feel cold, set against a typical whole-house insulation quote (blown-in or spray foam often runs several thousand dollars). 1) Door weatherstripping and a door sweep ($10-$25 a door): the biggest felt draft in most homes. 2) Foam gaskets behind outlet and switch covers on outside walls ($5 for a pack). 3) Caulk the window and door trim where it meets the wall ($5-$10 a tube). 4) Window insulation film kits ($15-$25 for several windows) or heavy thermal curtains. 5) Weatherstrip and insulate the attic hatch or pull-down stairs ($20-$50 DIY, $50-$150 for a ready-made stair cover): often a big hole in the ceiling. 6) Seal the basement rim joist with canned foam ($5-$10 a can). 7) Close the fireplace damper or fit a chimney plug ($30-$80) only once the fire and embers are completely out and cold, usually the next day; never on a gas-log fireplace (its damper must stay open); take the plug out before every fire; never block a flue a furnace or water heater vents into. Honest framing: these stop air leaks, which cause most of the 'cold house' feeling; they don't replace insulation in a truly empty wall or attic, and an energy audit (often discounted by utilities) shows which you need. 'Look Like a Scam' compares the price, never accuses a contractor or implies insulation quotes are actual scams. Safety: keep foam and film away from heat sources and flues; with an older natural-draft furnace or water heater, tightening the house can cause CO backdrafting, so get a combustion safety test and keep CO alarms working.

## 13. NEVER Plug Your Space Heater Into These 5 Things (It's How House Fires Start)

**1. Idea**
- Format: F1
- Built from: "The clever feature that makes cheap heaters safe — and why they're actually dangerous" · 2,950,819 (Technology Connections, Feb 2025)

**2. Thumbnail**
- Reference:
  - Technology Connections, "The clever feature that makes cheap heaters safe — and why they're actually dangerous" (3.0M views): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HnMuNCl7tZ8
- Keep: The small white space heater, big and bright.
- Change: The heater's plug is in a white power strip that is scorched black and partly melted, a thin wisp of smoke. Roger leaning in beside it, serious, pointing. Red circle on the melted spot.
- Text: Red band: FIRE STARTS HERE
- Don't: Flames or a house on fire (keep it believable).

**3. Video brief**

A 1,500-watt space heater draws about 12.5 amps for hours, above the 12 amps a 15-amp circuit is meant to carry continuously (the electrical code's 80% rule for continuous loads), so the weak point is the connection, not the heater. Never plug it into: 1) an extension cord (many light-duty household cords, 16- and 18-gauge, are rated only 10-13 amps and heat up, especially coiled or under a rug); 2) a power strip or surge protector (not built for continuous heater loads; most heater manuals say so); 3) a multi-plug adapter or cube tap; 4) a smart plug or timer not rated for the full load (check the rating; many are not); 5) a loose, worn or discolored outlet (a loose plug heats at the contacts and melts). Plug it straight into a wall outlet, ideally on a circuit with nothing else big on it, and check the plug after an hour: warm is normal, hot is not. Also: keep 3 feet clear of anything that can burn, never run it while sleeping or away, and look for tip-over and overheat shutoff and a safety listing (UL, ETL or CSA). Fact for the script: space heaters (fixed and portable) were involved in about one-third of US home heating fires but 88% of home heating fire deaths (NFPA, 2016-2020 annual averages). CPSC, the Red Cross and ESFI all say plug directly into the wall.

**1. Idea**
- Format: F17

**2. Thumbnail**

  - Ray Dutton's Garage, "The $10 Part HVAC Companies Built Their Summer On" (50K views): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t2ux1p6JdKA
- Keep: The small part held up big to the camera, curved red arrow onto it, the person reacting beside it.
- Change: The part is a furnace flame sensor (a thin metal rod on a small bracket): half covered in black soot, half polished shiny. Roger beside it, eyebrows raised.
- Text: NOT BROKEN (BROKEN in yellow)
- Don't: Their words (THIS SHOULD BE ILLEGAL).

**3. Video brief**

The part is the flame sensor: a thin metal rod in the burner flame that proves to the control board the gas lit. When it's coated with carbon or oxide, the board can't read the flame, so the furnace lights, runs a few seconds, shuts the gas off and retries, sometimes locking out after three tries. A new sensor costs about $10-$25; a service call to clean or replace it typically runs $100-$250, $300 or more after hours. The fix: turn off power at the furnace switch and turn the gas valve off (and let it cool), remove the burner door, take out the one screw holding the sensor, pull it out by its base, polish the rod lightly with a Scotch-Brite pad or very fine emery cloth (not coarse sandpaper; keep grit off the ceramic), wipe it, reinstall, restore power and watch a full cycle. If it still shuts off, other causes: a dirty filter (high limit), a clogged condensate trap or vent pipe (pressure switch), a weak igniter, or a bad sensor wire; that's when to call a tech. Safety: never touch the hot-surface igniter next to it (fragile, skin oils crack it); if you smell gas, stop and call the gas company. Prices as rounded ranges.

## 15. You've Been Running Your Ceiling Fan WRONG All Winter — Flip This Switch Tonight

**1. Idea**
- Format: F6
- Built from: "Ceiling fans: the simple idea we keep screwing up" · 3,632,065 (Technology Connections, Jun 2026)

**2. Thumbnail**
- Reference:
  - Technology Connections, "Ceiling fans: the simple idea we keep screwing up" (3.6M views): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_KWdCqpXB7A
- Keep: The ceiling fan big in the frame, warm cozy lighting.
- Change: Look up at the fan's motor from below; Roger reaching up, his fingertip on the small direction switch. One red arrow onto the switch. Snow visible through a window behind.
- Text: WARMER ROOM
- Don't: Their words; a fan spinning in summer light.

**3. Video brief**

Most ceiling fans have a small direction switch on the motor housing (or a reverse button on the remote). In summer the fan should turn counterclockwise looking up, pushing air down for a cooling breeze. In winter, switch it to clockwise on the lowest speed: it pulls air up and pushes the warm air that collects at the ceiling back down the walls without a draft. The U.S. Department of Energy recommends this winter setting. It helps most in rooms with high or vaulted ceilings, where the air near the ceiling can be several degrees warmer than at the floor; in a low-ceiling room the gain is small. The payoff: the room feels warmer at the same thermostat setting, so many people can set it a degree or two lower (the DOE's setback figure works out to roughly 1% a year per degree for 8 hours a day; keep the claim soft, never 'cuts your bill'). How: turn the fan off, wait for it to stop, flip the switch, run it on low. Costs: nothing. Turn it off when nobody's in the room. The brief follows DOE and ENERGY STAR guidance; check the reference video's own view of reverse mode before quoting it. Clean the blades first, since a winter's dust gets thrown around.

## 16. Why Buy a Generator? Your Car Can Keep Your Furnace Running in an Outage

**1. Idea**
- Format: F30
- Built from: "Why Buy A Generator? You Have One Parked In Your Driveway." · 2,450,035 (The DIY HVAC Guy, Oct 2025)

**2. Thumbnail**
- Reference:
  - The DIY HVAC Guy, "Why Buy A Generator? You Have One Parked In Your Driveway." (2.5M views): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qx3cC1y23JY
- Keep: The car in the driveway with the person beside it pointing.
- Change: Snowy driveway, car hood open, a small inverter box clipped to the battery, an orange outdoor cord running far off toward the house in the distance. Roger beside the car pointing at the inverter. Car well away from the house, no garage in the picture.
- Text: Red band: NO GENERATOR
- Don't: The car near or inside a garage (carbon monoxide).

**3. Video brief**

In a winter outage, a gas furnace won't run without power for its blower, inducer and igniter, but it only needs about 300-800 watts. A car's battery and alternator can supply that through a pure sine wave inverter (about $100-$250 for a 1,000-1,500 watt model) clamped to the battery, with the engine running to keep it charged; a hybrid can run for many hours on little fuel (an idling car typically burns roughly a quarter to half a gallon an hour). The furnace has to be connectable: most are hard-wired, so an electrician installs a furnace power inlet or single-circuit transfer switch (often $200-$500) that lets you plug it in safely; never backfeed a house panel or wire around a switch. Safety above all: the car runs outside in open air, never in a garage, even with the door open, because of carbon monoxide; park with the tailpipe pointing away from the house, at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents (CPSC's distance for generators), keep the tailpipe clear of snow, never sit or sleep in the idling car; bring the cord in without leaving a gap (close the window or door on it and seal the gap, or use the inlet the electrician fits); CO alarms on every level; an outdoor-rated cord of the right gauge. Honest limits: it runs the furnace, not the whole house; a modified-sine inverter can damage some furnace boards, and some furnaces won't light on an inverter whose neutral isn't bonded to ground (the flame sensor needs that reference, and polarity must be right): the electrician fitting the inlet can sort it out. Compare: a portable generator runs $500-$1,500 plus fuel and storage, a standby generator $7,000-$15,000+ installed.
